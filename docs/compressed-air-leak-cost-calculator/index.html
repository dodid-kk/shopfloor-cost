How it works

Where the presets come from

The hole-size presets are typical flows at about 100 psi (7 bar) for a rounded orifice, based on US Department of Energy compressed air leak tables. Measured flows from an ultrasonic survey are always better than estimates.

Why leaks are expensive

Compressed air is one of the most expensive utilities in a factory: a compressor turns only a small share of its electricity into useful air power. Leaks waste that air around the clock, often 20–30% of a compressor's output in plants without a leak programme, and they are silent enough to ignore until someone puts a number on them.

Worked example

A plant with 20 leaks the size of a 1/16-inch hole, about 4 CFM each at 100 psi, loses 80 CFM. At a typical 20 kW per 100 CFM that is 16 kW of compressor power.

Getting the inputs right

  • Flow per leak: an ultrasonic leak detector gives the best estimate. The presets are typical flows at about 100 psi; at lower pressure, leaks lose less.
  • Specific power: modern rotary screw compressors use roughly 16–22 kW per 100 CFM at full load. Part-load operation makes each leaked CFM more expensive, not less.
  • Hours: use the hours the system is pressurised, not the hours production runs. Many systems stay pressurised over weekends.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a 1/4-inch air leak cost?

At 100 psi a 1/4-inch leak passes roughly 60 CFM. At 20 kW per 100 CFM, 6,000 hours and $0.12 per kWh that is about $8,600 a year.

How do I convert l/s to CFM?

Multiply litres per second by 2.119. 10 l/s is about 21 CFM.

What percentage of compressed air is lost to leaks?

Surveys commonly find 20–30% in plants without a regular leak programme, and under 10% in well-managed systems.

Should I lower the system pressure too?

Yes. Leak flow falls with pressure, so reducing pressure by even 1 bar cuts leak losses and compressor energy.
